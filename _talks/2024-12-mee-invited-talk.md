## Abstract

The development of needle-based, minimally invasive technologies holds significant promise for advancing the treatment of middle ear pathologies. Such innovations require a comprehensive understanding of the biomechanics of the tympanic membrane (TM), particularly its behavior during needle insertion and cutting. This presentation explores the mechanical and fracture-related properties of the TM, laying the groundwork for the design of safer and more effective surgical devices. 

We began our investigation with experiments on synthetic membranes to assess fundamental mechanical properties and validate preliminary models. Following this, we conducted experiments on real TMs to explore their cutting behavior and fracture-related mechanical properties in detail. Using a 2-cycle needle insertion/extraction process on the gerbil TM, we measured forces and analyzed energy components, including fracture energy, friction energy, strain energy, and hysteresis loss, to understand the biomechanics of the process. Our findings demonstrated the viscoelastic nature of the TM, with fracture and friction energies being the dominant contributors to energy dissipation, while hysteresis loss had minimal impact. Notably, the puncture force of the TM remained stable shortly after the animal’s death but increased after a week due to tissue drying effects. Needle geometry was found to significantly influence key parameters such as crack length, insertion forces, and puncture displacement, while insertion velocity only affected insertion forces. We also computed the fracture toughness of the gerbil TM, determined to be 0.33±0.10 kJ/m2. 

Building upon these experimental findings, we developed numerical models to simulate TM mechanics. Initially, we used a combination of a 3D finite-element (FE) model of the TM and middle-ear structures and a 2D cohesive zone FE model of needle insertion. The 3D model employed a nonlinear Ogden material formulation to capture the hyperelastic behavior of the TM and provided insights into whole-domain deformation and stress distribution during needle interaction. The 2D cohesive zone model was used to extract cohesive parameters of the TM, enabling detailed analysis of its mechanical response during puncture. 

Taking this work further, we introduced a more advanced 3D FE model incorporating hyperelastic-plastic damage mechanics for a comprehensive analysis of TM puncture. This novel model captured the geometric complexity of the middle ear and needle, as well as the TM’s nonlinear material properties, including high-strain deformation, crack propagation, and damage accumulation. By optimizing fracture-related properties such as yield stress and fracture strain, the model accurately replicated experimental puncture forces, predicting a maximum von Mises stress of 1.0 MPa at the puncture instance. 

The advanced model also enabled us to evaluate key factors affecting needle-TM interactions, such as insertion location, needle inclination, needle tip geometry, and TM thickness, on puncture forces and deformation. Furthermore, it accounted for both axial and lateral forces during insertion, providing valuable insights for the development of haptic devices, surgical robots, and virtual-reality medical training systems. 

This work significantly advances our understanding of TM puncture and damage behavior during needle insertion. The methodologies and findings presented provide a robust scientific basis for developing innovative, minimally invasive surgical tools and training platforms, contributing to improved treatments for hearing and balance disorders
